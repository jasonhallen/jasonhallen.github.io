Report: Rangefinder Experiences

May 3, 2026

It started with an interest in smaller, simpler 35mm film cameras. I've since acquired six different rangefinder cameras. Sears 35|RF, Yashica Electro 35 GSN, Yashica Lynx 5000E, Konica IIIA, Canon 7s, and Petri Racer. I'll tell you about each of these cameras and why I find myself drawn to rangefinders more than SLRs.

Sears 35|RF

I didn’t expect the Pentax MX to feel so liberating. It was small enough to carry around in a pocket, light in the hand, and quick to dial in the exposure. Nothing fancy got in the way. So of course I wondered how shooting with an even smaller and simpler camera would feel.

This led me to the fixed-lens compact rangefinders that were popular in the 60s and 70s. After reading up on the various models I decided on a Ricoh 500G for two particular features.

  • It has both an automatic exposure mode and a manual mode.
  • Its light meter works in both modes. Often in this class of camera the light meter only works in automatic exposure mode, not in manual mode.

[IMAGE OF SEARS 35|RF]

The Ricoh 500G was also rebranded and sold as the Sears 35|RF. You can often pick up the Sears 35|RF for cheaper than the Ricoh 500G, so that's what I did. My Sears 35|RF arrived fully operational and in good condition. I cleaned it up, put in new light seals, popped in a new battery, tuned the variable resistors in the light meter circuit, and adjusted the infinity focus. Good to go.

Features

  • 40mm f/2.8 fixed lens
  • Leaf shutter
  • 1/8s - 1/500s shutter speeds
  • f/2.8 - f/16 aperture
  • Shutter speed priority auto exposure mode
  • Manual exposure mode
  • No parallax correction, just parallax indicator lines

Photos

I had only shot with SLRs before, so I spent the first roll getting used to focusing and composing with a rangefinder. Here are a few shots from that roll which was Lomography Berlin Kino 400.

From this first roll I sensed something special about the photos coming out of this camera. It was hard to put my finger on it. The compositions seemed stronger. See, I don't like to crop my photos. I want to see the full compositions framed by the dark borders around the negatives. With that self-imposed constraint, my photos live and die by how well I framed the composition I had in mind while snapping. With the Sears 35|RF my photos seemed to have a higher-than-average rate of winners. Or maybe this was just in my head?

I shot another roll, this time Kentmere Pan 400.

This only confirmed my suspicion that the photos were coming out better composed. I was hooked on this camera. Here's what I liked about the Sears 35|RF, which also applies to the Ricoh 500G.

  • Simple
    • Fixed lens
    • Mechanical
    • Leaf shutter
  • Cheap
    • $40
  • Discrete
    • Small, pocketable, light
    • Unobtrusive
    • Quiet
  • Approximate
    • Focusing is quick and dirty
    • No depth of field preview, depth of field scale, parallax correction
    • Estimation of focus and composition

I went on to shoot several more rolls on the Sears 35|RF on trips to Atlanta, Des Moines, and Indiana. Here's a sampling of photos from rolls 034, 035, 039, 041, and 046.

This is one of my favorite cameras. It's perfect for an around-the-town or travel camera because it's so small. I nails focus, framing, and exposure, and the lens is great. I don't feel like I'm sacrificing any image quality when shooting on this.

The downsides

  • The focus, shutter speed, and aperture rings are packed tightly on the lens barrel, so it can be awkward to adjust
  • Can forget to use the parallax indication lines

Yashica Electro 35 GSN

My coffee shop buddy Nate heard that I was shooting film and fixing up cameras. He asked if I could fix his old Yashica Electro 35 GSN that he had in the 1970s. It had stopped working years ago, so he bought a second one. He said if I could fix his old one then I could have his other one.

Fixing his Electro 35 was easy. The wire going out of the negative terminal of the battery compartment had corroded and become disconnected. I soldered it back in place and it worked just fine. I then took possession of his other Electro 35.

Features

  • 45mm f/1.8 fixed lens
  • Shutter speed range
  • Shutter speed priority exposure mode
  • No manual exposure mode

I've shot three rolls on the Electro 35. Two of them were black and white which I've developed. The third was Ektar 100, which I haven't developed yet.

The photos coming out of the Electro 35 blew me away. The lens is the star of the show. My God, the photos are beautiful. However, there was one big flaw.

Downsides

  • No manual exposure control. Forget using an incident meter.
  • Metering is janky with two-stage travel of the shutter release button
  • It's big but whatever

Yashica Linx 5000E

The ideal Electro 35. Manual exposure control. Better metering ergonomics. Smaller, cuter.

Previous generation before the Electro 35.

Downsides

  • I had strange colors and exposures with this.
  • Spent a lot of time repairing it but the shutter blades bound and dented

Konica IIIA

Considered one of the greatest rangefinder and viewfinders of all time

059 071

Mine had a very gummy winding lever. It made shooting the first two rolls annoying. The lever would bind up. I stripped this down to clean it out. Eventually I got it working consistently, but now I'm distrustful of it.

Fast shutter speeds are also too slow, so I have to remap the speeds when I shoot with it.

I want to love this camera. The viewfinder and advancement lever are so nice. The optics are great. But I don't trust it.

Canon 7s

A true joy. I briefly had a Canon P but it broke. I didn't dig the framelines anyway, so I was happy to return it.

My first interchangeable lens rangefinder. Focal plane shutter.

This is an incredible camera. The only downside is that the meter doesn't work.

Petri Racer

I saw a photo of this camera online. It totally enchanted me. Bought it cheap off eBay. Worked really well, even the meter.

  • Leaf shutter
  • Manual exposure mode

074 076

Downsides

  • Have to advance the film before meter turns on
  • Pain in the butt to calibrate the rangefinder and get good focus
  • Easy to accidentally move the ISO setting and throw off the meter
  • No parallax correction, only indicator lines
